# Legitimate interest assessment: the persistent browser id

GDPR Article 6(1)(f). Assessed 2026-08-13 by Marc Meijers.

Covers `evtrip.cid`: a v4 UUID the browser generates and stores in
`localStorage`, sent as `X-Client-Id` and stored hashed on `app_events` and
`trip_stats`. See `frontend/lib/analytics.ts` and `src/app/core/visitor.py`.

**Outcome: proceed on legitimate interest, with the mitigations below. There is
a known and deliberately accepted gap against ePrivacy Art 5(3), set out in full
at the end. This document exists so that decision is on the record rather than
implicit in the code.**

## 1. Purpose test

The question is "does anyone come back?". It is the difference between a busy
afternoon after a Reddit post and a thing people actually use, and it decides
whether the project is worth continuing to run and pay for.

It cannot be answered any other way here. The other pseudonym, `visitor_hash`,
is salted with the current UTC date specifically so that it cannot be followed
past midnight. That design is deliberate and is not being loosened. It counts
heads honestly and makes retention structurally unanswerable, so a second,
stable identifier is the only route to the answer.

The interest is real, present and specific, not speculative. It is our own
interest rather than a third party's.

## 2. Necessity test

Could the purpose be achieved with less?

- **Server derived id.** Rejected. Deriving from IP plus user agent would be worse for the visitor, not better: it would apply to everyone with no way to refuse and could not be cleared, whereas a browser generated id disappears when site data is cleared.
- **Aggregates only.** Does not work. Repeat visits are the question; no aggregate over a nightly-rotating pseudonym can recover it.
- **Shorter lifetime.** The window is 15 months on `trip_stats` and 90 days on `app_events`. This is a holiday app, so the cycle is annual and anything under about thirteen months cannot answer "did last August's users come back this August". 15 months is the shortest window that answers the actual question.
- **A third party analytics product.** Strictly worse on every axis: a script from another origin, a profile held by a company whose business is profiles, and the loss of the privacy page's claim that no third-party scripts load.

Minimal in scope as well as time. The id rides `planTrip` and the event endpoint
only. It is deliberately absent from `apiFetch`, `getTrip` and the run
endpoints, so opening a link somebody sent you is never recorded against a
pseudonym. Share links get forwarded to people who never chose anything, and
attaching an id to reads would turn the share token into a record of who was
sent it. `tests/test_client_id.py::TestTripStats` guards this.

## 3. Balancing test

**What is actually held.** A random number with nothing derived from the person,
stored as a SHA-256 hash salted with `SECRET_KEY`. Against it: a route pattern,
a coarse device bucket, a country, and on `trip_stats` a geohash-4 corridor. No
name, no email, no account, no precise location, no cross-site data.

**Reasonable expectations.** A visitor to a free trip planner does not expect to
be tracked across sites, and is not. Whether they expect the same site to know
they have been before is more marginal, which is why the privacy page has a
section titled "One thing that does remember you" rather than burying it.

**Impact.** Low. The id cannot be linked to an identity by us or by anyone who
obtains the database without also holding `SECRET_KEY`. It cannot follow anyone
to another site. The worst realistic outcome, a database disclosure, exposes
that some pseudonym planned some corridors, which is a much smaller harm than
the trip coordinates sitting in the same breach.

**Power balance.** No dependency, no lock-in, no service withheld from anyone
who opts out. Counting off changes nothing about how the app works.

**Children.** Not directed at children and unlikely to attract them. No age
gate, which is proportionate given the data involved.

**Conclusion.** The interest is not overridden. The impact is low, the data is
pseudonymous by construction, and refusing is free and effective.

## 4. Mitigations relied on

Each of these is load-bearing. Removing one means redoing this assessment.

1. Generated by the browser, never derived from the request, so clearing site data genuinely ends it.
2. Stored only as a salted hash, so an id leaked into a URL or a screenshot cannot be used to find that person's rows.
3. Validated against a strict v4 pattern and discarded otherwise, so a public endpoint cannot be turned into free-form storage.
4. Never sent on reads of a shared trip.
5. Expires: 90 days on `app_events`, 15 months on `trip_stats`, where the column is nulled and the corridor row kept.
6. GPC and Do Not Track are honoured, and no id is minted at all for those visitors.
7. An explicit opt-out on `/privacy#counting` that stops the events and deletes the stored id immediately.
8. Disclosed in plain language on the privacy page, under its own heading, with the right to object stated.

## 5. The ePrivacy gap, stated plainly

Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive, implemented in the Netherlands as
Telecommunicatiewet 11.7a, requires prior consent for storing or accessing
information on a user's device unless it is strictly necessary to provide the
service the user requested. It is technology neutral, so `localStorage` is
covered exactly as a cookie would be.

Retention analytics is not strictly necessary. **Writing `evtrip.cid` therefore
requires opt-in consent that this app does not collect.** GPC, DNT and the
toggle are opt-out mechanisms, and opt-out is not consent. The Dutch supervisory
authority's tolerance for privacy-friendly first-party analytics is narrower
than the CNIL's published exemption, and neither obviously extends to a 15 month
cross-visit identifier.

A GDPR legal basis and an ePrivacy consent requirement are separate obligations,
and satisfying the first does not discharge the second. Sections 1 to 4 above
address the first only.

**Decision.** Accept the risk and keep the identifier. The alternatives were a
consent prompt, which contradicts the entire design of a site whose distinguishing
claim is that it needs no banner and which would leave the metric too thin to be
useful, or dropping retention analytics entirely and losing the answer to the
only product question worth asking before deciding whether to keep running this.

**Exposure.** The realistic worst case is an instruction to obtain consent or
stop, most likely following a complaint, since no supervisory authority is
scanning hobby projects. There is no meaningful fine risk at this scale for a
first-party, non-commercial, non-profiling identifier that honours opt-out
signals. The residual harm to visitors is low for the reasons in section 3.

**Revisit if** the app becomes commercial, starts running ads, shares corridor
data containing the id with anyone, adds accounts, or grows to a scale where it
is plausibly on a regulator's radar. Any of those changes the calculation, and
the consent prompt becomes the right answer.

## Review

Annually, or on any change to what the id is attached to or how long it is kept.
